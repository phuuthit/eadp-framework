Equitable Adaptation Development Pathways

A seven-step framework for planning climate adaptation that is fair to different groups, not only effective on average. MSc thesis by Phuu Thit Kyaw, Wageningen University & Research, in partnership with Deltares.

How the framework was built

A qualitative study in two phases. Systematic literature reviews gave the evidence base for a first draft. Interviews with experts who build and apply pathway approaches then tested and refined it.

Phase 1Three systematic literature reviews
Draft frameworkFive steps
Phase 2Ten semi-structured expert interviews
Refined frameworkSeven steps, full adaptation cycle

Phase 1: three systematic literature reviews

Following the PRISMA guidelines. Scopus and Google Scholar; research and review articles from 2020 onwards (2018 for review 3, which found fewer than 10 papers otherwise); environmental science; English; search terms applied to titles, abstracts and keywords. Screening in two stages: abstracts, then full text.

Review 1: framework steps

What are the common steps, and their order, in existing adaptation pathway frameworks?

"adaptation" AND "pathways" AND "climate"

Review 2: tools for distributional impacts

What tools are used to assess the distributional impacts of climate challenges?

"distributional" AND "impact*" AND "assessment" AND "climate"

Review 3: tools for evaluating measures

What tools are used to design and evaluate adaptation strategies, with a focus on distributional impacts and equity?

"distributional" AND "impact*" AND "assessment" AND "climate" AND "adaptation" AND ("measure*" OR "strateg*")

Steps for the draft were chosen from the elements used most often across the reviewed frameworks. Each tool from reviews 2 and 3 was rated High, Medium or Low on two criteria: whether it suits co-creation with diverse stakeholders, and whether it directly supports the framework. Only tools rated High went into the draft.

Phase 2: expert interviews

20experts contacted
10interviewed
~1 hourper interview, online
Apr–May2025

Experts in adaptation pathways, participatory methods and equity research, from Deltares, Wageningen University & Research and other organisations. Before each interview, they received the draft framework, the questions, and a hypothetical case applying it. Questions covered eight themes: overall perception, strengths, weaknesses, steps, tools, equity integration, criteria for the framework, and applicability.

Interviews could not be recorded under Deltares policy, so notes were taken and sent back to each expert within 24 hours to check. Only validated notes were analysed: first the common points, then contrasting views, then a list of changes.

What the experts said

7 of 10 liked the idea or found it useful, mainly for its explicit focus on equity.

6 of 10 valued giving local people a voice through participatory methods.

8 of 10 questioned whether it would work in practice, and suggested testing it on a real case.

All 10 said it should cover the full cycle, including implementation, monitoring and evaluation.

4 of 10 found some terms confusing, such as the academic names of justice principles.

What changed

5 additions

  1. Explain clearly how EADP differs from DAPP and CRDAPP.
  2. Cover the full cycle, with monitoring that checks whether outcomes are equitable.
  3. Offer multiple entry points into ongoing projects.
  4. Include ways to identify conflicts, especially where power is unequal.
  5. Use scenarios to test how pathways perform in different futures.

6 modifications

  1. Define equity more clearly and apply it consistently.
  2. Rename steps to match established pathway approaches.
  3. Redesign the framework diagram for clarity.
  4. Describe each tool's benefits, limits and complexity.
  5. Use plain names for justice principles.
  6. Stay consistent with the terms of other pathway approaches.

Changes were accepted if they fit the theory and the scope of the thesis. Two were left for future research: detailed tool descriptions and entry points into ongoing work.

Draft pathway diagrams (Appendix D)

The draft drew each group's pathways as a tree. On expert advice, the refined framework uses a metro map instead, like other pathway approaches, which also shows no-regret measures (see Step 4 on the wheel).

Limits and next steps

Limits

  • The framework has not yet been tested in a real case. This is its most significant weakness.
  • Who makes the final decision on implementation is outside its scope. If groups have no real say, it risks repeating the top-down process it aims to prevent.
  • The feedback mostly reflects a European research context. Experts from other regions, including some working in the Mekong Delta, were contacted but did not respond or were unavailable.
  • The reviews found fewer than 50 papers, partly because they started from 2020. The tools are options, not fixed recommendations.

Next steps

  • Apply EADP in a case study to explore how it works in practice.
  • Describe each tool in detail: its benefits, limits and complexity.
  • Show different entry points for using EADP in ongoing work.
  • Develop a clear method for Step 7 to check whether outcomes are actually equitable.
EADP does not promote one single “right” equitable outcome. It supports a process where communities and decision-makers can see how different pathways lead to different outcomes, and decide based on that.
